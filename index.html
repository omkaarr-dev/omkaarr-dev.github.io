SipSure

Know What You Drink.

A handheld colorimeter, with an app, that tests drinking water for chromium, nitrite, chlorine and lead. Colorimeters from Hach, Hanna and Thermo cost lab money. We're running the same reagent chemistry on much cheaper parts. Five of us are building it as team Rano for the Xylem Water Hackathon 2026–27, and I'm on the device hardware and firmware.

Chromium is calibrated in the lab. Testing on the device is next.

How it measures

The reagent changes the sample's colour, and the more contaminant there is, the deeper the colour gets. We shine light through the cuvette and the photodiode measures how much makes it out the other side. From that we get absorbance, and the calibration curve we made in the lab turns it into mg/L.

Try the slider. A darker sample lets less light through, and absorbance goes up in a straight line with concentration.

The four tests

Each one uses a published colour reaction, so anyone can check our numbers against the standard method. We also wanted fluoride, but we didn't have the reagent, so it's out of the first version.

Why two boards

The main ESP32-S3 does the measuring and runs the 1.3-inch OLED and buttons. A second ESP32-S3 with a camera photographs the same cuvette against a white backlight, so each reading comes with a picture we can check it against. Readings go to the app with a location attached, and we plan to send them to the government's water-quality database.

Before building anything I simulated the circuit in my own fork of the Velxio simulator.

ARGO

Parcel pickup at the campus gate, checked by face.

Our ILGC-II project. ARGO logs every parcel that comes in at Plaksha's Gate 1 and checks the student's face when they pick it up. I was project manager for our team of six, Team Helios. We built the app with Google Antigravity, an AI coding agent. I worked out the system design and user flows, then tested and debugged what it produced.

Finished as a working prototype

Why face verification

We surveyed students, and most of them preferred a face scan to a QR code, so that's what pickup uses. Gemini 2.5 Flash reads the label on each parcel so nobody has to type it in. If it can't read one, the guard enters it by hand.

If a parcel isn't collected, the student gets another reminder every 48 hours.

How the boards talk

All the boards on the rover share one pair of wires, using CAN. Each message has an ID. If two boards send at the same moment, the one with the higher-priority ID goes through and nothing gets corrupted. Cars use the same bus for the same reason.

My part was the Teensy 4.x firmware for the drive system. It sends and receives these messages with FlexCAN_T4 and reads the wheel encoders. I also ran some basic servo PWM tests on an STM32G474RE.
